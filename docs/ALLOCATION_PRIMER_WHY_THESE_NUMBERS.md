### Why 70% Equities?

The case is time-tested math:

- The **equity risk premium** (excess return over T-bills) has averaged **~5.0% annualized** from 1928–2025 (Damodaran NYU dataset). Over a 10-year horizon, compounding at 5% above the risk-free rate is the single best-known mechanism for growing capital.
- The **S&P 500** has delivered approximately **10.5% nominal CAGR** from 1957–2025. At that rate, $1 becomes $2.72 in 10 years. Fixed income has averaged ~4–5% nominal — the same $1 becomes $1.63.
- **Time-horizon protection:** equity volatility drops significantly over longer windows. The probability of a 10-year holding period being negative in the S&P 500 since 1926 is approximately 6%. The probability of a 1-year holding being negative is roughly 26%.
- **Institutional benchmark:** Vanguard's 80/20 portfolio is the reference for "aggressive growth." 70/30 is the recognized balanced-growth anchor. A 70% equity allocation lands squarely in the growth tier without going to the extreme end.

**Why not 80% or higher?** The structural policy cap is 80%, and 70% is chosen because the remaining 30% in other asset classes meaningfully reduces drawdown severity without significantly sacrificing long-term return. Moving from 70% to 80% equity adds roughly +0.3–0.5% expected annual return but increases worst-case drawdown from ~35% to ~45% — a poor trade for most investors.

**Why not 60%?** A 60% equity allocation is the classic "balanced" (not growth) mandate. It is appropriate for a 15–20 year retiree horizon with capital preservation priority. For a 5–10 year growth horizon, 60% gives up too much compounding.

### Why 20% Fixed Income?

Fixed income in a growth portfolio is not primarily about *earning* returns — it is about **not losing catastrophically** and **having dry powder to buy equities cheaply during crashes.**

- **Volatility reduction:** US aggregate bonds reduced portfolio volatility by approximately **25–35% during equity drawdown periods.** In 2008–2009, a 100% equity portfolio fell ~50%. A 70/30 portfolio fell ~35%.
- **Sharpe ratio improvement:** The historical 60/40 portfolio Sharpe ratio is approximately **0.45** — superior to pure equity on a risk-adjusted basis over most 10-year windows.
- **Rebalancing mechanism:** When equities fall 30%, a 20% bond allocation gives you a concrete pool of assets to sell at a premium (bonds typically rise in equity crashes) and redeploy into equities at discounted prices. This rebalancing effect is worth an estimated **0.5–1.0% annualized** over full cycles.
- **2022 exception:** 2022 was unusual — both equities (-19%) and bonds (AGG -13%) fell together due to the fastest rate-hiking cycle in 40 years. This is the known failure mode of fixed income, and why it is capped at 20% rather than the traditional 30–40%.

**Why not 10%?** Too little to meaningfully dampen volatility or provide a rebalancing reserve. The cushion effect requires mass.

**Why not 30%?** At 30% bonds, you are leaving roughly $0.5–0.7% annualized compound return on the table over 10 years. On a $1M portfolio, that is approximately $50K–$75K in forgone growth. The marginal risk reduction from 20% to 30% bonds does not justify that cost on a growth mandate.

### Why 4% Digital Assets?

Digital assets are treated as a **high-risk / high-reward satellite.** The logic is asymmetric payoff:

- Bitcoin's 2019–2021 adoption cycle: +1,300%. Ethereum: +6,800%.
- A **4% allocation that goes to zero** costs you 4 points of total portfolio — survivable.
- A **4% allocation that returns 10x** adds 36 points to total portfolio — transformative.
- The hard governance cap is **8%**. The 4% target sits at exactly half the cap, leaving room for tactical upside.
- Institutional legitimacy has arrived: BlackRock (iShares IBIT), Fidelity (FBTC), and major pension funds have established digital asset exposure, removing custody risk as a blocker.

**The risk is real:** Bitcoin had drawdowns of **70–80%** in 2018 and 2022. Ethereum fell over 90% in 2018. A 4% allocation at peak, falling 75%, leaves you with 1% — a 3 point drag. Painful but not portfolio-destroying. Anything larger would be.

### Why 4% Commodities?

Commodities solve one problem equity and bonds cannot: **inflation protection in commodity supercycles.**

- Gold delivered **+25% in 2022** when both equities (-19%) and bonds (-13%) fell simultaneously. It is the only major asset class that commonly decorrelates in that specific macro regime.
- The 2000s commodity supercycle (GSCI: +150% from 2001–2008) provided positive returns while US equities had a flat-to-negative decade.
- A **3–5% commodity allocation** improves portfolio Sharpe ratio in inflationary regimes (PIMCO commodity diversification research).
- Within commodities: Gold gets 50% (most reliable crisis hedge), Energy gets 30% (oil-price inflation hedge), Broad Basket gets 20% (full cycle coverage).

### Why 2% Cash?

Cash is the structural floor — not an investment choice but an **operational requirement:**

- T-bill yields are currently **4–5%**, which partially offsets cash drag. Holding cash is not as costly as it was in 2010–2021 when rates were near zero.
- The 2% cash floor is the **governance-enforced minimum.** It ensures there is always liquidity available for tactical rebalancing, opportunistic deployment, and transaction costs.
- Holding more than 2–3% cash in a growth portfolio is a drag. Cash drag vs equities over 10 years at a 5% equity risk premium = 50 basis points per year per additional cash point.

### Why 72% US Equities (within equities)?

- The **US represents ~60% of global equity market capitalization** (MSCI ACWI, 2025). The 72% target is already an overweight vs global benchmark.
- US equities **outperformed MSCI EAFE** (International Developed) by approximately **2.1% annualized from 1970–2025** (MSCI data, dividends reinvested). That gap compounds enormously over 10 years: $1 at +10.5% → $2.72; $1 at +8.4% → $2.24. That is a 21% difference in ending wealth.
- **Earnings quality:** US GAAP accounting, robust shareholder return culture (buybacks + dividends), and highest density of technology/platform companies with durable competitive moats.
- **SIH data advantage:** This system's replay intelligence is most sophisticated for US equities, meaning targets here are most dynamically adjustable over the monitoring period.

**Why not 80–90% US?** That is home-country concentration. The policy enforces a **minimum 10% international** for structural diversification. At 72% US, we are already at a substantial overweight; going higher creates single-country macro risk.

### Why 20% International Developed?

- **Valuation arbitrage:** International developed markets (Europe, Japan, Australia) trade at significant P/E and price-to-book discounts vs the US. As of 2025, S&P 500 P/E ~22 vs MSCI EAFE ~14. Historically, these valuation gaps mean-revert, generating excess returns for patient holders.
- **Correlation benefit:** US-EAFE rolling 3-year correlation is approximately **0.75–0.85** — correlated but not perfectly so, providing diversification value.
- **Currency diversification:** EUR, JPY, GBP exposure hedges against USD weakness cycles (e.g., 2002–2007 when USD fell 40% vs EUR, adding returns to unhedged international holdings).

**Why not more?** International developed has structurally underperformed US by 2%+ annualized for the past 15 years. A 20% allocation is the minimum meaningful exposure to maintain diversification without over-betting on mean reversion.

### Why 8% Emerging Markets?

- **EM = ~12% of global market cap** (MSCI ACWI). The 8% target is a slight *underweight* vs global benchmark — intentionally, to account for higher risk.
- **Long-term growth premium:** EM GDP growth has historically been **2–3x developed market rates**, providing higher return potential over 10+ year horizons.
- **Risk acknowledgment:** Currency volatility in EM is substantially higher than developed markets. Political and regulatory risk is real (e.g., Chinese regulatory crackdowns on tech in 2021 wiped 70%+ from peak).
- **No current SIH replay coverage** for EM — targets are methodology-baseline only and will remain less dynamically adjusted until data coverage improves.

**Why not 15–20%?** EM carries risks that warrant a modest allocation in a risk-managed portfolio. The 8% provides meaningful long-term growth exposure without concentration in high-risk geographies.

### Why 45% Mega Cap (within US equities)?

- **Market reflection:** mega-cap US companies represent approximately **45–55% of the S&P 500 by market weight** as of 2025. This allocation mirrors market reality — it is not a bet, it is a recognition of where economic value resides.
- **Quality factor:** Mega-caps are characterized by strong free cash flow, high return on equity, pricing power, and durable competitive moats. Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Google, and Amazon collectively generate free cash flow in the hundreds of billions annually.
- **Drawdown resilience:** In the 2022 bear market, the S&P 100 (mega-cap proxy) fell approximately 19%, very close to the broader S&P 500's 19.4% — but recovered faster in 2023 with the AI-driven multiple expansion.
- **Full SIH replay chain** is available for mega-cap US equities — the highest-fidelity dynamic adjustment capability in the entire allocation system.

### Why 30% Large Cap?

- Large cap ($85B–$565B) is the **proven compounding tier** — established businesses with lower single-stock concentration risk than mega.
- This forms the core of the S&P 500 ex-mega, capturing the bulk of US economic activity in a diversified manner.
- Lower alpha ceiling than mega (no AI infrastructure moat), but also lower catastrophic risk.

### Why 15% Mid Cap?

- **Historical outperformance:** The S&P MidCap 400 has outperformed the S&P 500 on a risk-adjusted basis over most 20-year rolling windows — the "sweet spot" thesis: companies large enough to be institutionally covered and liquid, but small enough to have meaningful growth runway.
- Mid-cap companies are often the **emerging leaders** — today's large/mega caps were yesterday's mid caps (e.g., Salesforce, ServiceNow).
- Higher beta than large cap means it performs best in economic expansion, which aligns with the growth mandate.

### Why 7% Small Cap?

- The **Fama-French size premium** documents a ~2% annualized return premium for small-cap over large-cap over long periods.
- **Important caveat:** This premium has been absent for stretches of 5–10 years, particularly 2010–2020. The 7% allocation is modest — it participates in size premium cycles without overcommitting to a factor that requires patience.
- Small caps have a lower correlation to mega/large, improving diversification at the margin.

### Why 3% Micro Cap?

- Governance cap enforced at **5% maximum micro cap** of total portfolio. The 3% target sits well within bounds.
- Micro cap is the **highest-risk tier** — illiquid, limited analyst coverage, wide bid-ask spreads, high failure rate.
- The 3% allocation is intentionally small: it provides optionality on outsized gain cycles (micro cap outperformed dramatically in 2020–2021 post-COVID recovery) without creating material downside risk.

## How These Numbers Stay Current: The Monitoring Loop

The 5–10 year view is not a "set and forget" structure. The recalculation engine monitors:

1. **Replay evidence:** rolling 90-day relative returns, outperformance persistence rates, and volatility penalties for HIGH-sophistication nodes (all US equity tiers and subtiers)
2. **Evidence weight formula:**

$$\text{evidence\_weight} = \text{outperformance\_persistence} \times (1 - \text{volatility\_penalty})$$

   If evidence weight > 0.52, a reallocation is proposed; the direction and magnitude depend on whether evidence weight is above or below 0.5.

3. **Maximum drift:** any single recalculation is capped at ±3.0% (governance policy), and recalculations must be at least 14 days apart.
4. **Validator suite:** 8 automated validators check hierarchy sum invariants, policy bounds, concentration ceilings, and evidence alignment before any commit is allowed.

The allocation numbers in this document are the *starting point*, seeded from the institutional evidence basis in the methodology YAML. Over the 5–10 year horizon, as replay evidence accumulates, the system will propose — and the portfolio owner will approve or reject — incremental adjustments within the governance rails.
